The full story

I

Rosario

Lionel Andrés Messi was born on June 24, 1987, in the Las Heras district of Rosario, Argentina's third-largest city. His father, Jorge Messi, worked as a steel factory supervisor at Acindar in nearby Villa Constitución. His mother, Celia Cuccittini, managed a magnet workshop. It was a working-class household where football was not a pastime but a language — spoken fluently by every member of the family.

Messi's older brothers, Rodrigo and Matías, played regularly, as did his cousins Maximiliano and Emanuel Biancucchi, both of whom would eventually become professional footballers. Leo, the youngest of the active players, grew up chasing their touches in the narrow streets of Las Heras. By the time he was three, he had a ball at his feet constantly. At four, he joined Grandoli, a local club coached by his father. By five, he was already playing in organised matches against children a full year older.

In 1995, at age seven, Messi joined Newell's Old Boys, one of Rosario's two major clubs and the team his family had supported for generations. He was placed in a youth cohort born in 1987 that would become legendary within the club: "La Máquina del '87" — the Machine of '87. Over six years at Newell's, Messi scored nearly 500 goals across all age groups, an almost incomprehensible tally for a child who was consistently the smallest player on every pitch. During half-time of first-team matches, he would entertain the crowd by performing ball tricks, juggling with a fluency that made the adults stop and watch. His idol was the Brazilian striker Ronaldo — the player he grew up watching and, in his own account, the best forward he had ever seen.

By the time he was seven, he was already playing against children a full year older — and winning.

II

The Diagnosis

When Messi was ten years old, a pediatric endocrinologist delivered a diagnosis that threatened to end his footballing future before it had truly begun: growth hormone deficiency. Without treatment, Messi would not grow to a size that could sustain a professional career. The therapy required daily injections into his legs, a treatment that cost at least 1,000 Argentine pesos per month — a significant sum for a working-class family in the late 1990s.

Newell's Old Boys initially agreed to help cover the costs, but later reneged on their promise. Jorge Messi's health insurance covered only the first two years of treatment. The family was in a difficult position: the therapy was working, Leo was growing, but the financial burden was unsustainable. Meanwhile, Buenos Aires club River Plate had been scouting Messi. Their playmaker Pablo Aimar was the player Messi admired most, and River Plate offered to sign him and pay for his medical treatment in full. It seemed like the perfect solution — until Newell's refused to release his player registration card, blocking the transfer entirely.

For two years, Messi continued his treatment while his future hung in limbo. He injected himself every night, the pain of the needles becoming routine. As he later recalled: "At the beginning it was painful, but sometimes certain things must be endured in order to achieve your dreams." The boy who would become the greatest footballer alive was, at twelve, fighting a battle that had nothing to do with goals or trophies — a battle against his own body, against bureaucracy, against the cruel arithmetic of poverty and medicine.

"Sometimes certain things must be endured in order to achieve your dreams."

III

The Napkin

In September 2000, the Messi family arranged a two-week trial with FC Barcelona. Lionel, now 13, flew for only the second time in his life. The 13-hour flight was turbulent; he could not sleep and felt too sick to eat. When he arrived at El Prat airport, Horacio Gaggioli — the agent facilitating the trial — was waiting. He had seen video footage of Messi's skills at Newell's, but what he saw stepping off the plane gave him pause: Gaggioli has since described the boy as exceptionally small, and doubted for a moment he could play football at all.

That same day, despite his exhaustion, Messi trained with Barcelona's youth team. The reaction in the dressing room was disbelief — Cesc Fàbregas has recalled that the group wondered how such a small, silent boy could hold his own, since Leo barely spoke at first. Gerard Piqué, over a foot taller, has said the room had written the kid off before he touched the ball. Then he touched it, and the doubts were gone.

Carles Rexach, Barcelona's technical director, was initially in Sydney watching the Olympics but arranged to evaluate Messi upon his return. He has said he saw enough in two minutes and told the board the club had to sign the boy immediately. But the Barcelona board was unconvinced. They questioned whether such a shy child — teammates called him "El Mudo," The Mute — could adapt to a foreign country. They worried about the cost of his growth hormone therapy. Weeks passed. The Messi family grew restless.

On December 14, 2000, Rexach met Gaggioli and fellow agent Josep Maria Minguella for lunch at the Pompeia Tennis Club. Gaggioli warned he would take the boy elsewhere — the family, he said, had even started to consider Madrid. Rexach panicked. With no contract paper at hand, he grabbed a paper napkin from the table and a pen from a waiter. He scribbled, in blue ballpoint, an agreement taking full personal responsibility for signing Lionel Messi. It was signed by Rexach, Gaggioli, and Minguella. The most consequential contract in football history was written on a cafeteria napkin. It was later auctioned for nearly $1 million.

In two minutes, Rexach had seen enough — the board had to sign the boy, he told them.
IV

The Prodigy

In February 2001, Messi's family relocated to Barcelona, moving into an apartment near Camp Nou. He enrolled at La Masia, Barcelona's famed youth academy. The first year was brutal. A transfer conflict with Newell's meant he could play only in friendlies and the Catalan league — no official competition. Without football, he struggled to integrate. He was so quiet that some teammates genuinely believed he was mute. At home, his mother Celia, unable to adapt to life in Spain, moved back to Rosario with his brothers and younger sister María Sol. Lionel stayed with his father. The homesickness was immense.

But the talent could not be suppressed. After his registration paperwork cleared in 2002, Messi was enrolled with the Royal Spanish Football Federation and could play in all competitions. In his first full season, he scored 36 goals in 30 games for the Cadetes A team, leading them to an unprecedented treble: the league, the Spanish cup, and the Copa Catalunya. In the Copa Catalunya final, a 4–1 victory over Espanyol, Messi broke his cheekbone in a collision. He was allowed to play on wearing a protective plastic mask — the "partido de la máscara," the final of the mask. When the mask became a hindrance, he tore it off and scored two goals in ten minutes.

Arsenal offered to sign him. He chose to stay. By sixteen, he was called up to train with the first team. His unofficial debut came in a friendly against Porto; his family found out he was playing by watching the news. On October 16, 2004, at age 17, he made his official La Liga debut against Espanyol, coming on as a substitute in the 82nd minute. He was the youngest player to represent Barcelona in an official match. The boy from Rosario who had arrived weighing barely as much as his suitcase was now a professional footballer at one of the world's biggest clubs. The napkin contract had become the most important document in the history of sport.

V

The Captain

The arc from prodigy to leader is the arc of Messi's entire career, and it was never guaranteed. For years, he was accused of not caring enough about Argentina — of being a Barcelona player who happened to wear the blue and white, rather than an Argentine who happened to play for Barcelona. The criticism stung because it contained a grain of truth: the weight of a nation's expectations sat differently on his shoulders than it had on Diego Maradona's. In 2014, he carried Argentina to the World Cup final in Brazil, only to lose to Germany in extra time. In 2016, after a Copa América final defeat to Chile, he announced his international retirement — the national team, he said, was over for him. He was 29.

He reversed the decision months later, driven by something he could not articulate — an unfinished feeling, a debt owed to the shirt. What followed was the most remarkable phase of his career. In 2021, he captained Argentina to Copa América glory, ending a 28-year major trophy drought for the national team. In Qatar in 2022, he finally won the World Cup, lifting the golden trophy in a black bisht as millions watched from Buenos Aires to Barcelona. In 2024, he led Argentina to back-to-back Copa América titles, cementing a dynasty that will outlive his playing days.

And then, on August 31, 2026, after a World Cup final loss to Spain, Lionel Messi sat down with a pen and a notebook. He wrote by hand the words he could not say in a press conference, posted a photograph of the page to Instagram, and closed the book on 193 caps, 125 goals, and an era that redefined what one person could achieve in a team sport. The napkin contract boy from Rosario had given everything for the shirt. And the shirt, in the end, was enough.
